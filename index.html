Overview

The idea in one minute

Conventional assay

Grind the sample, digest it in acid, run reagents and spectrometry. Accurate — but the portion tested is destroyed.

This system

Shine stable light on the whole sample and record its reflectance spectrum. The food is untouched and can still be sold, planted or eaten.

  1. Scan

    The sample moves through a sealed cabinet while the camera records a full spectrum at every pixel — not just red, green and blue.

  2. Predict

    Bonds in water, starch, protein and fat absorb at known wavelengths. Models trained on laboratory assays turn those fingerprints into concentrations.

  3. Map

    Because every pixel carries a spectrum, the answer is drawn back onto the sample as a map — not one averaged number for a whole batch.

Why this matters the longer version

Reference methods for food composition need reagents, laboratory infrastructure and trained analysts, and they return a single figure for a homogenised batch. That figure hides the variation between individual tubers, grains or plates — which is exactly the variation a grader, breeder or quality officer needs to see.

A hyperspectral camera instead records a three-dimensional hypercube: two spatial axes describing the sample, and a third axis of wavelength running from 400 to 1000 nm. Composition leaves a fingerprint in that spectrum, so the same measurement can be repeated on many individual items, quickly and at low marginal cost.

What the screen is doing

The cabinet carries a Raspberry Pi touchscreen running an app called Hyperspectral Remote Display, with two tabs: Selection and Results.

  1. Pick a system

    Proximate, Mineral or Vitamins — each one loads its own set of calibrated models.

  2. Pick the food

    Seven modes: yam, plantain, chicken, beans, jollof, beef and potato.

  3. Read the result

    Predicted values appear on the Results tab in the assay's own units — percent for proximate, mg/kg for minerals.

Instrument

A sealed cabinet, a moving stage, one line of light at a time

Illumination, geometry and sample position are fixed inside the enclosure so that every scan is repeatable — the precondition for comparing a spectral reading against a laboratory value.

  1. Illuminate

    The sealed cabinet is flooded with stable broadband VIS–NIR light, so the only thing changing between scans is the sample.

  2. Translate

    A stepper motor advances the linear stage, sweeping the platform — and the food on it — beneath the camera at a controlled rate.

  3. Capture a line

    At each position the camera records one spatial line, with a full reflectance spectrum for every pixel along it.

  4. Stack the hypercube

    Successive lines are assembled into the data cube: two spatial dimensions plus wavelength, covering 400–1000 nm.

  5. Calibrate and predict

    Reference calibration removes illumination and sensor effects. Chemometric regression — including gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) — maps spectra to concentrations learned from paired laboratory assays.

  6. Map and report

    Predictions are rendered back onto the sample as a spatial–spectral map, alongside fit statistics: R², adjusted R², RMSE and MAE.

Results tab

Predicted values are listed for the chosen system and mode, each in the units of its reference assay: moisture, protein, lipid, fibre, ash, carbohydrate and energy (kcal) for proximate analysis; Na, K, Fe, Ca and Se in mg/kg for minerals.

The status line reports which saved capture was restored — for example “Restored .tif: Low Fried plantain” — so a reading can always be traced back to the scan it came from.

How to read these strips

The left half is the image reconstructed from the hyperspectral cube; the right half is a false-colour spectral map, where colour encodes relative spectral response across the measured bands. Warm and cool regions mark spectrally different material — not nutrient concentration directly. Concentrations come from the calibrated models in the results section.

Checked against the laboratory

Every model was validated against reference laboratory assays on the same samples. Ten nutrient targets across yam, Irish potatoes, beans, chicken and beef.

RMSE is the typical size of a prediction error, weighting large misses more heavily; MAE is the average size, without that extra weight. Adjusted R² penalises model complexity, so a model cannot score well simply by having more predictors. RMSE and MAE carry the units of each assay — yam minerals in mg/kg, beef vitamins in mg/100 g — so they compare models within a target, not across targets. “—” marks a statistic not reported in the source figure.

Uses

Where a non-destructive reading is useful

Because the sample survives the measurement, the same reading can be taken on items still going to be sold, planted, cooked or exported — and on many of them.

Post-harvest grading

Sorting tubers and grains by composition rather than size or appearance, without cutting into the produce being graded.

Quality assurance & labelling

Verifying declared nutrient content on packaged foods as a rapid screen ahead of confirmatory laboratory tests.

Biofortification screening

Screening breeding lines for mineral density while keeping the seed or tuber viable for the next generation.

Cooked-food assessment

Measuring what remains after boiling, frying or cooking — as shown here on jollof rice, fried plantain and boiled yam.

Process monitoring

Repeatability from the sealed cabinet and motorised stage makes it practical to track composition through a production run.

Research & teaching

A contained platform for chemometrics, spectral calibration and machine-learning coursework.
